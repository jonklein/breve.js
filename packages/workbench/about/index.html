About breve

All models are wrong, some are useful. (George Box)

A simulation is a model, and every model leaves things out. breve is built around that: it is a place to build a model quickly, see what it does, change what you think is wrong, and compare the versions, rather than a place to find the one right answer.

Principles

  • Nothing is hard-wired. Rules are small scripts, bodies and worlds are JSON, and searches are plug-ins. The engine only knows how to hold state and step time.
  • Everything can be changed while it runs. Edit a rule, move a slider, poke one agent, or swap its controller.
  • Runs are values. A simulation can be copied mid-run, replayed exactly from a seed, and run beside others to compare.
  • Defaults do the boring parts. A scene with almost nothing in it still runs, draws and can be inspected.

What is in it

Free agents (flocks, ants, herds), cellular grids and patch worlds, networks, and articulated bodies with physics; neural networks and language models as controllers; sweeps and searches over parameters; and an assistant that proposes checked changes in plain language. It runs in the browser, and a headless mode runs the same scenes from the command line.

Where it comes from

breve began as a C++ toolkit for 3D simulations of multi-agent systems and artificial life, with behaviors written in Python or a small scripting language called steve, plus physics, collision detection and an OpenGL display. It was written to support research on artificial life and evolutionary computation, and went quiet around 2009. It was described in breve: a 3D simulation environment for the simulation of decentralized systems and artificial life (Artificial Life VIII, MIT Press, 2002) and, later, in the chapter 3D Multi-Agent Simulations in the breve Simulation Environment (Klein & Spector, Artificial Life Models in Software, 2nd ed., Springer, 2009). This is a rebuild for the web: it runs in the page, with no install.
